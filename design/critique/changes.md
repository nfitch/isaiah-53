## Verse 2

### v2-2 יַּעַל (yaʿal): body

- Before: Root: עָלָה (ʿalah). To go up, ascend, spring up. KJV renders it as future tense but the Hebrew is past tense — he came up, he sprouted. The image is of something pushing upward from below.
- After: Root: עָלָה (ʿalah). To go up, ascend, spring up. KJV renders it as future tense, reading the passage as prophecy, but the Hebrew form is the narrative past (wayyiqtol) — he came up, he sprouted. The image is of something pushing upward from below.
- Why: Hebrew has no tense in the English sense; the form is wayyiqtol, which normally narrates past events. This keeps the point while explaining why KJV chose the future.

### v2-4 יּוֹנֵק (yoneq): body

- Before: Root: יָנַק (yanaq) "to suckle, to nurse." A yoneq is literally a nursling — something still drawing life from its source. "Tender plant" is an interpretation. The image is of something newly alive and utterly dependent.
- After: Root: יָנַק (yanaq) "to suckle, to nurse." A yoneq is literally a nursling — something still drawing life from its source. Applied to plants the word means a young shoot or sucker, so "tender plant" is a fair rendering, but it loses the nursling sense. The image is of something newly alive and utterly dependent.
- Why: יוֹנֵק is attested in a botanical sense (cf. יוֹנֶקֶת, "shoot," Job 8:16), so calling the KJV rendering merely "an interpretation" is unfair. / The word 'image' now appears twice in a row.

## Verse 3

### v3-4 אִישִׁים (ishim): body

- Before: plural of אִישׁ (ish) — not the generic word for humanity (אָדָם, adam) but specifically men of standing, prominent men, notable persons. The word carries weight — he is abandoned not just by people generally but by those who matter, the significant ones.
- After: plural of אִישׁ (ish) — a rare plural, rather than the generic word for humanity (אָדָם, adam). Some read it as men of standing, prominent men — abandoned not just by people generally but by those who matter; others take it simply as "men."
- Why: The "men of standing" reading is one disputed view, not settled lexical fact. As written, the entry builds commentary on top of it.

### v3-5 אִישׁ (ish): body

- Before: here the singular form, meaning a man in the specific sense — a particular individual of standing.
- After: here the singular, in a construct phrase — "a man of pains," a Hebrew idiom that characterizes a person by what marks him.
- Why: Nothing in אִישׁ מַכְאֹבוֹת implies standing; the real nuance is the "man of X" construct idiom.

### v11-7 דַעְתּוֹ (daʿto): body

This is not intellectual knowledge but deep, personal, lived knowing. By his intimate knowing — of God, of suffering, of humanity — he shall justify many. The instrument of justification is his profound experiential knowledge.
- After: Root: יָדַע (yadaʿ) as in verse 3 — intimate, experiential knowledge, knowing from the inside. The suffix וֹ means "his." By his intimate knowing he shall justify many. "His knowledge" can also be read as the knowledge of him that others have.
- Why: "Of God, of suffering, of humanity" and "instrument of justification" are interpretation. The real ambiguity (subjective vs. objective genitive) is missing.

### v11-8 יַצְדִּיק (yatsdiq): body

- Before: Root: צָדַק (tsadaq) "to be righteous, to be just, to justify, to declare righteous, to vindicate." A causative form — he shall cause many to be righteous, he shall declare many righteous, he shall make many just. Not merely pronouncing them innocent but causing them to be righteous — a transformative act, not only a legal declaration.
- After: Root: צָדַק (tsadaq) "to be righteous, to be just, to justify, to declare righteous, to vindicate." A causative form — he shall cause many to be righteous, he shall declare many righteous, he shall make many just. Both a legal declaration and a making righteous fall within the word's range; the Hebrew does not decide between them.
- Why: Taking a side in the forensic vs. transformative debate is theological opinion.

### v11-9 צַדִּיק (tsaddiq): body

- Before: Root: צָדַק (tsadaq) as above. The adjective righteous, just, right. He is called my righteous servant — the same root used in יַצְדִּיק. The one who is righteous causes others to be righteous. The word play is intentional — righteousness flows from him who possesses it.
- After: Root: צָדַק (tsadaq) as above. The adjective righteous, just, right. He is called my righteous servant — the same root used in יַצְדִּיק. The one who is righteous causes others to be righteous. The two words sit side by side — yatsdiq tsaddiq — a sound play English loses.
- Why: "Righteousness flows from him" is commentary. The sound play is the linguistic point.

## Verse 12

### v12-5 רַבִּים (rabbim): body

- Before: Root: רַב (rav) as in verse 11 — many, great, numerous. KJV renders it "the great" here but the word is the same rabbim — the many — as in verse 11. The shift to "the great" in KJV introduces a distinction not present in the Hebrew. He is given his portion among the many — the same multitude whose iniquities he bore.
- After: Root: רַב (rav) as in verse 11 — many, great, numerous. KJV renders it "the great" here. רַב can mean great as well as many, and the parallel with עֲצוּמִים (the mighty) supports "the great" here; but it is the same word KJV renders "many" in verse 11 and later in this verse.
- Why: "Great" is within רַב's range and fits the parallelism, so saying KJV adds a distinction overclaims. The line about the same multitude is interpretation. / The surviving sentence still asserts the old 'the many' claim, which contradicts and duplicates the new text after it.

### v12-7 אֶת (et): body

- Before: direct object marker, straightforward.
- After: here the preposition אֵת "with" — with the mighty. KJV "with" is accurate.
- Why: אֶת־עֲצוּמִים is "with the strong"; the object of יְחַלֵּק is שָׁלָל.

### v12-7 אֶת (et): rendering

- Before: (empty)
- After: with
- Why: Preposition "with," not the object marker.

### v12-11 תַּחַת (takhat): body

- Before: Root: תַּחַת (takhat) "under, beneath, in place of, instead of, because of, in exchange for." A rich preposition. KJV renders it "because" but the word carries the sense of in the place of, in exchange for, as a substitution for. The reward comes in exchange for, as a substitution for what follows — his self-pouring unto death. The substitutionary force of תַּחַת is stronger than mere causation.
- After: Root: תַּחַת (takhat) "under, beneath, in place of, instead of, because of, in exchange for." A rich preposition. KJV renders it "because." Joined with אֲשֶׁר, תַּחַת אֲשֶׁר is an ordinary idiom for "because"; but the root sense — in the place of, in exchange for — remains audible beneath it.
- Why: תַּחַת אֲשֶׁר is a standard causal idiom. Claiming "substitutionary force" is theological reading presented as grammar. / The surviving sentence contradicts the revision that follows it and repeats its substitution senses.

- Why: נִמְנָה is passive and takes no direct object; אֶת־פֹּשְׁעִים means "with transgressors."

### v12-19 פֹּשְׁעִים (posheʿim): body

- Before: Root: פָּשַׁע (pashaʿ) as in verses 5, 6, and 8 — willful rebels, those who deliberately revolt. The plural participle — the ones actively rebelling. He was numbered with those in the very act of rebellion.
- After: Root: פָּשַׁע (pashaʿ) as in verses 5 and 8 — willful rebels, those who deliberately revolt. The plural participle — the ones actively rebelling. He was numbered with the rebels.

### v12-29 יַפְגִּיעַ (yafgiaʿ): body

- Before: Root: פָּגַע (pagaʿ) as in verse 6 — to meet, to encounter, to strike, to fall upon, to intercede. In verse 6 this same root was used of YHWH causing our iniquity to strike the servant — the LORD made iniquity collide with him. Now at the chapter's close the same root appears again — but now the servant himself makes intercession, causes a meeting on behalf of the rebels. The word that described violence done to him now describes his own action for others. The one who was struck now intercedes. The root פָּגַע bookends the chapter's central act — struck on their behalf, he now pleads on their behalf.
- After: Root: פָּגַע (pagaʿ) as in verse 6 — to meet, to encounter, to strike, to fall upon, to intercede. In verse 6 this same root was used of YHWH causing our iniquity to strike the servant — the LORD made iniquity collide with him. Now at the chapter's close the same root appears again — but now the servant himself makes intercession, causes a meeting on behalf of the rebels. The word that described violence done to him now describes his own action for others. The one who was struck now intercedes. The verb is imperfect — he intercedes, or will intercede — where KJV uses the past "made intercession."
- Why: This removes the homiletic close and adds the tense point KJV flattens.
